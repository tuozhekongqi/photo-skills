# Photo Zine Social - Prompt Library v4.0

Use these prompts as **construction templates**, not rigid scripts. Replace bracketed placeholders where helpful. Always analyze the actual photo before finalizing the prompt.

---

# 0. Universal preamble

Use this before any style-specific prompt when working from a real user photograph:

> Use only the currently selected source photo. Do not combine it with other uploaded images. First analyze the photo's subject, strongest geometry, depth, light, palette, negative space, and scene mood. Preserve its identity and key spatial relationships. Any added illustration, typography, paper treatment, abstraction, or surreal element must derive from this photo. Keep the result clean, restrained, social-media ready, and clearly connected to the original photograph.

---

# A. Travel Ticket / Souvenir Pass

> Based on the uploaded real photograph, create a refined travel-souvenir design that combines authentic photography with one collectible ticket / destination-pass element. Preserve the source subject, composition, light, and color as much as possible. Use warm ivory, cream, or pale neutral paper; elegant serif or refined uppercase typography; subtle paper thickness; restrained microcopy; and generous whitespace. The ticket should feel literary and collectible rather than official or commercial. Extract the ticket's motif, illustration, and palette directly from the source photo. Add one short English headline and one smaller travel line. Avoid fake airline/train realism, excessive stamps, barcode clutter, heavy vintage distress, sticker-book decoration, and bright commercial tourism styling.

Optional copy tone:
- `WANDERLUST`
- `MORNING TRIP`
- `ALONG THE RIDGE`
- `A small trip, a long memory.`

# B. Watercolor Travel Editorial

> Reinterpret the uploaded travel photograph as a refined watercolor travel-editorial page. Keep the scene immediately recognizable: preserve the main subject, landmark relationships, and original visual rhythm. Let selected edges and secondary regions dissolve into transparent watercolor wash, light colored-pencil texture, or soft paper bleed while the focal subject stays more precise. Use a low-saturation palette derived from the source image, generous breathing room, quiet paper texture, and restrained magazine-like typography. Add a 1-3 word English title and one short handwritten English line if the composition benefits from text. Avoid children's-book illustration, overly cute rendering, dense brush detail, saturated postcard colors, and tourism-brochure graphics.

# C. Minimal Collage Poster

> Turn the uploaded photograph into a minimal editorial collage poster. Preserve one strong photographic hero area and use only one or two additional graphic interventions: a clean crop, paper plane, negative-space block, source-derived geometric shape, or tightly controlled secondary crop. Let the source geometry determine the layout. Use generous whitespace, matte paper texture, a restrained source-derived palette, and small modern typography. Keep the poster elegant, quiet, and visually disciplined. Do not use scrapbook stickers, random tape, decorative icons, multiple torn scraps, giant commercial type, or repeated collage templates.

# D1. Second World Photo Editorial - Standard

> Create a 3:4 vertical "Second World" photo editorial based on the uploaded real photograph. Divide the composition into two balanced zones, approximately 1:1, but let the exact boundary follow the photo's own visual logic rather than a mechanical horizontal cut.
>
> Preserve the real photograph faithfully in the photographic zone: do not change the main subject, human actions, spatial relationships, natural color, or light beyond necessary cropping and subtle cleanup.
>
> In the paper-space zone, use a warm off-white paper background with generous whitespace. First identify the source photo's most distinctive structural element — for example a road, waterline, reflection, bridge, cable, wall edge, roofline, cloud layer, shadow, mountain ridge, stair, branch, or human movement. Ask: if that structure were physically usable in another world, what would someone naturally do with it? Could it be pulled, opened, climbed, carried, measured, stitched, repaired, connected, unfolded, poured, collected, or entered?
>
> Build the second-world concept from that answer. The photographic cut shape must participate in the idea; do not default to an ordinary torn-paper edge. You may add 0-3 minimal black hand-drawn line figures, but only if they genuinely use or interact with the transformed structure. They are users, not decoration.
>
> Add one brief handwritten English aside derived from the actual scene. Tone: natural, light, observant, slightly playful or poetic. No motivational quote, no generic slogan, no repeated formula. Keep the whole piece bright, quiet, restrained, handmade, independent-magazine-like, and conceptually inevitable.
>
> Avoid commercial advertising, decorative clutter, dense figures, fixed tear templates, arbitrary stars/leaves, cartoon styling, or redesigning the photograph into a different scene. The result should make the viewer think: "Of course — the original photo could be read this way."

# D2. Second World - Strong Interaction

> Use the uploaded photograph to create a conceptual second-world editorial with one clear physical interaction. Preserve the original image faithfully. Identify one structural feature in the photo that can plausibly become usable: bridge, road, ridge, cable, water surface, wall, stair, shadow, window, cloud band, reflection, or edge. Transform only that feature into an actionable second-world object. Add 1-3 minimal black line figures performing one specific action such as pulling, climbing, measuring, connecting, repairing, carrying, unfolding, or entering. Keep the action simple and visually legible. Use warm paper, strong negative space, restrained typography, and one short handwritten English aside. Avoid cute poses, repeated stock actions, arbitrary surrealism, or decorative doodles.

# D3. Second World - No Figures

> Create a quiet second-world reinterpretation of the uploaded real photograph without any illustrated people. Preserve the photographic subject and spatial logic. Let one source structure — a waterline, shadow, ridge, road, roof edge, reflection, branch, cloud, or wall — continue into a minimal paper-space construction using cut shape, line, fold, measurement mark, or abstract extension. The concept should feel discovered inside the original image rather than added afterward. Use warm off-white paper, generous whitespace, minimal text, and one soft handwritten English line if useful. Keep the result elegant, restrained, and conceptually clear.

# E. Soft Analog Diary

> Preserve the uploaded photograph almost entirely and use only a subtle analog travel-diary character when the source or user intent supports it. Keep highlight rolloff gentle; grain, color drift, and print softness are optional and minimal rather than default effects. Add at most one small diary gesture such as a handwritten note, tiny date-like notation, underline, or minimal margin caption. The result should still read first as the original photograph, not as a film preset. Avoid heavy grain, sepia, fake dust, light leaks, sticker-book decoration, or over-cleaning.

# F. Museum Catalogue

> Present the uploaded photograph as if it were a carefully reproduced artwork or cultural study in a museum catalogue. Preserve the image faithfully and place it within a generous white, ivory, or warm-neutral field. Use one refined title, one small subtitle or catalogue-style line, and restrained editorial spacing. The layout should feel archival, studied, and quietly prestigious. If the photo is a cultural detail, carving, roof, gate, tree, stone, or ornament, frame it as an object of attention rather than a tourist attraction. Avoid fake museum logos, fabricated accession numbers, dense explanatory text, ornate borders, or luxury-brand styling.

# G. Light & Negative-Space Poetry

> Use the uploaded photograph to create a minimal poetic image in which light, air, distance, shadow, sky, water, mist, reflection, or empty space becomes the main design material. Preserve the source image's natural quietness and do not fill the frame with extra graphics. Allow generous breathing room. Use subtle tonal shaping, soft paper or clean photographic space, and either no text or one very small line placed where the image naturally leaves room. Avoid collage clutter, strong graphic borders, oversized typography, decorative motifs, or artificial drama. The image should feel calm enough to pause on.

# H. Cinematic Travel Still

> Treat the uploaded photograph like a still frame from an intimate travel film. Preserve the real scene, subject, and location. Use a cinematic crop only if it strengthens the existing narrative; shape contrast and color subtly; keep highlights natural; and let atmosphere, distance, weather, or movement carry the frame. Optional typography should be tiny and secondary, like a chapter title rather than a movie poster. Avoid blockbuster teal-orange grading, fake film logos, billing blocks, excessive letterboxing, artificial lens flare, or dramatic changes to the scene.

Use refined serif / sans typography, clean margins, and subtle paper or print softness. The page should feel like a high-quality magazine feature, not a tourist brochure. # L. Detail Collectible Card

> Extract one meaningful detail from the uploaded photograph and turn it into a refined collectible visual card. Choose a detail that genuinely carries the identity of the place or scene: carving, ceiling medallion, lantern, roof ornament, plaque, stone texture, window, ripple, bridge arch, tile, or other distinctive motif. Keep enough context for the crop to remain meaningful. Present it on a clean paper field with one short title / specimen label and minimal supporting text. Use restrained border, shadow, or paper thickness only if needed. Avoid arbitrary cropping, decorative sticker treatment, fake rarity marks, or commercial trading-card aesthetics.

# Batch prompt - inventory + deduplicate + route styles

> Treat the uploaded originals as one batch in upload order. Keep source identity and avoid a heavyweight visible per-image audition. Collapse only truly near-identical same-angle duplicates when allowed; do not discard different viewpoints for visual-story redundancy. Choose treatment from source fit and approved user anchors, without style quotas. Default to one source per finished image. Multi-source collage is optional only for complementary same-subject frames with a real compositional reason; never use nine-grid/grid-mosaic/contact-sheet dumping, and never let collage cover the key subject. Run one light final batch check.

# Batch prompt - "next batch"

> Continue only from sources still marked pending or redo. Do not regenerate done images. Do not substitute visually similar images. Respect the near-duplicate decisions already made. Do not force style variety. Repeating a treatment is acceptable when the sources genuinely support it. Avoid only obvious template repetition that weakens the photographs.

# Batch prompt - quality gate

> Before accepting each output, check: correct source, source identity preserved, no accidental multi-photo collage, no unrelated invented object, typography subordinate, style family clearly executed, palette still source-related, layout not generic, and no repeated template behavior. If any check fails, regenerate before marking the source done.


## Source Selector — conservative batch intake

> Preserve the uploaded batch in order. Do not use visual-story redundancy or low-value filtering as a workload shortcut. Collapse only truly near-identical same-angle frames when the user has allowed that. Different viewpoints remain separate sources even when they share a theme.

## Preference-aware selector

> Apply user feedback with scope-first, bounded updates. A strongly liked treatment is a compatible positive anchor, not a universal template. A rejected execution suppresses its concrete visual failure signature; do not ban the parent family unless the user explicitly establishes a durable family-level rejection or repeated cross-context evidence does so. Newness is not extra weight.

## Series Planner

> Sequence only after good local treatments exist. Rhythm is optional and should be achieved mainly by ordering, not by forcing style changes. No required quiet/strong alternation, no family/layout quotas, and no mandatory collage/experiment slot.
