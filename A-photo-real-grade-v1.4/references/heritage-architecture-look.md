# Heritage Architecture Look — 古建纪实质感

## Scope
Use for traditional Chinese architecture and related travel scenes: palace halls, red walls, glazed-tile roofs, eaves, dougong, plaques, stone balustrades, courtyards, axial spaces, corridors, brick walls, gates, and architectural details.

This is a **conditional look**, not a universal preset. Apply only when the source image already supports the relevant light, weather, geometry, and color relationships.

## Target character
Quiet, documentary, structured, tactile, restrained, and cinematic without looking generated.

The photograph should feel like a carefully exposed and graded architectural image, not a synthetic reconstruction.

## Color logic
- Preserve believable blue sky when it exists; do not manufacture blue sky from overcast weather.
- Let warm roof tiles, aged yellow-gold surfaces, red walls, gray brick, and stone separate naturally.
- Red walls should feel deep and stable rather than neon or oversaturated.
- Warm roof surfaces may be emphasized when real light supports them; avoid pushing everything toward orange.
- Neutral materials such as brick, stone, pavement, and balustrades should keep neutral variation and texture.
- Prefer local color separation over a global orange/teal cast.

## Tonal logic
- Keep eaves, dougong, corridors, and recesses meaningfully dark; shadow depth is part of the architecture.
- Dark does not mean blocked: retain enough internal structure to read texture and construction.
- Keep highlights controlled on stone, sky, roofs, and painted surfaces.
- Avoid HDR-style lifting that makes every plane equally bright.
- Use tonal hierarchy to emphasize architectural mass, repetition, and depth.

## Texture logic
- Preserve roof-tile rhythm, brick texture, painted timber details, carved stone, weathering, patina, and historical traces.
- Clarity should come from believable local contrast and source detail, not hard halos or AI-like sharpening.
- Do not smooth, reconstruct, repaint, or simplify aged surfaces.

## Composition reading
Prioritize the architecture before people.

Strong signals include:
- axial symmetry;
- centered facades;
- layered roofs;
- repeating eaves / rafters;
- diagonal roof lines;
- long corridors;
- stone-rail leading lines;
- large sky fields used as negative space;
- tight detail crops of eaves, tiles, plaques, painted beams, and roof ornaments.

For close architectural details, look for rhythm, repetition, geometry, shadow, and clean background separation rather than adding atmosphere.

## People semantics
People may remain when they provide scale, rhythm, or lived-in atmosphere without competing with the building.

Prefer to keep:
- small distant figures;
- back-facing walkers;
- edge-positioned figures;
- people that reinforce depth or scale.

Consider removing or reducing only when a person:
- dominates the frame;
- blocks the architectural subject;
- breaks a strong axis or geometric order;
- pulls attention away from the intended subject.

Do not empty a public heritage site merely to make it look 'clean'.

## Cinematic intensity
Cinematic feeling should come from:
- deeper but readable shadows;
- clear warm/cool relationships;
- structural repetition;
- controlled highlight roll-off;
- architectural scale;
- strong geometry;
- believable local light.

It should **not** come from:
- invented sunset;
- fake dramatic clouds;
- artificial light shafts;
- exaggerated teal/orange grading;
- synthetic haze;
- replacement skies;
- excessive vignette.

## Weather fidelity
Weather is part of source identity.
- Clear sky may stay clear and be refined.
- Thin cloud may be given more tonal separation.
- Overcast should remain overcast unless the user explicitly requests a weather transformation.
- Do not make an ordinary gray day look like late golden hour merely because warm architecture looks attractive.

## Common failure modes
Avoid:
- fake cyan / plastic blue sky;
- orange roofs with no plausible light source;
- neon red walls;
- crushed black eaves with no texture;
- global HDR microcontrast;
- oversharpened tile edges;
- reconstructed ornaments or signage;
- removing every visitor;
- exaggerated wide-angle distortion;
- letting dramatic sky become more important than the building.

## Decision rule
If the source already has strong architecture, good framing, and useful light, do not assume it needs a stronger grade. Use near-original/A-lite when that is enough.

Under the shared default route, a non-portrait heritage image still uses A+B. Keep the photographic foundation restrained; when visible editorial treatment adds value, let B express it, and when it does not, let B remain near-no-op. Do not switch to A-only merely because the photograph is strong, and do not force visible B treatment merely because the subject is heritage architecture.
